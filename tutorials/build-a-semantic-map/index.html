Build a Semantic Map

The Semantic 3D View turns your corpus into a navigable constellation. Every document becomes a glowing point in three-dimensional space: works that are stylistically or thematically similar drift toward each other, while outliers float on the periphery. This tutorial explains how to read the map, how to identify clusters, and how to move from a point in space to a passage in the reader.

Prerequisites

  • A completed corpus with the NLP pipeline run (see Getting Started).
  • At least five documents are needed for the point cloud to show meaningful structure; ten or more produces clearer clustering.

Understanding the layout

Before touching the controls, it helps to know what the view is showing and what it is not.

What positions represent
Each document is embedded using all-MiniLM-L6-v2, a sentence-transformer model that produces 384-dimensional dense vectors. These are reduced to three dimensions with UMAP. The UMAP projection preserves local neighbourhoods: if two points are close, the documents are genuinely similar in the high-dimensional space. Global distances (two clusters on opposite sides of the cloud) carry less information — they indicate dissimilarity, but not its degree.

What colour and glow represent
All points share the same default blue-violet colour at rest. The glow pulses slowly; this is a visual effect, not a data encoding. Hover a point to see a tooltip; click to select and expand a detail card. The selected point turns amber.

Corpus label
The bottom-left corner shows the active corpus name, document count, the embedding model, and the dimensionality reduction method.

Step-by-step

  1. Open the Semantic 3D view

    Click the Semantic icon in the top navigation bar. The canvas loads immediately. If no embeddings have been computed yet, an empty-state prompt appears — click through to the Corpus view to run the pipeline.

    The cloud starts rotating automatically. This auto-rotation helps you see the three-dimensional structure without needing to orbit manually.

  2. Orbit, zoom, and inspect

    The camera is controlled by the OrbitControls widget:

    GestureAction
    Left-dragOrbit (rotate camera around the cloud)
    Scroll / pinchZoom in and out
    Touch two-finger dragOrbit on mobile

    Pan is disabled by design — the cloud is always centred. Zoom is clamped between a very close distance (1.5 units) and a wide-field distance (20 units) so you cannot accidentally lose the cloud.

    Use the toolbar in the bottom-right corner to control rotation and zoom with buttons:

    • Pause / Play — stop or resume auto-rotation
    • Direction arrows — reverse the rotation direction
    • Zoom in / out — increment camera distance
    • Reset zoom — return to the default framing

  3. Hover to identify documents

    Move your cursor over any point. A compact tooltip appears in the top-right corner showing:

    • document title (truncated if long)
    • publication date
    • language code

    The tooltip follows focus — it updates as you move between points without clicking.

  4. Select a point for full metadata

    Click any point to select it. The hover tooltip is replaced by an expanded card showing:

    • title and authors
    • date, language (ISO 639-1 code expanded to full language name)
    • any custom metadata fields you added during import
    • filename and token count (or file size if the pipeline has not run)

    Click the Open in reader button at the bottom of the card to switch directly to the Close Reading view with that document loaded. Click the × button or click elsewhere on the canvas to dismiss.

    Tip

    Selecting a point pauses hover tooltips for other points until you dismiss the selection. This lets you examine a document card while you plan your next move.

  5. Enable clustering

    Click the Network icon (the rightmost button in the bottom-right toolbar) to activate DBSCAN clustering.

    When clustering is on:

    • Each point is assigned to a cluster or marked as noise
    • Cluster members are coloured in one of seven distinct hues; noise points remain the default blue-violet
    • Points in the same cluster pulse synchronously, making the groups visually distinct even when they overlap along the depth axis

    A cluster controls panel appears just above the toolbar, showing:

    • ε (epsilon) — a slider from 0.1 to 3.0. This is the DBSCAN radius parameter: the maximum distance between two points for them to be considered neighbours. Drag it left to find only tight, dense clusters; drag right to merge more spread-out groups.
    • Cluster count — the number of clusters found at the current ε value, updated in real time as you adjust the slider.

    Note

    DBSCAN requires a minimum of two points to form a cluster. Small corpora (fewer than ten documents) may produce zero clusters at low ε values — try increasing ε if you see “no clusters” with a reasonable-sized corpus.

  6. Interpret the clusters

    Clusters reflect stylistic proximity as captured by the embedding model. Common patterns across text corpora:

    • Author clusters — works by the same author often group tightly because an author’s vocabulary and syntax are consistent across titles.
    • Genre clusters — prose, essays, and poetry tend to occupy different regions of the space.
    • Period clusters — language shifts over time, so texts from different eras may separate even when topics overlap.
    • Theme clusters — texts dealing with the same subject matter may cluster regardless of author or period, though this effect is weaker than style.

    Note which works appear as noise (unclustered) — outliers are often the most interesting: a single anomalous work in a corpus, a text written in an unexpected register, or a translation that reads differently from the original.

  7. Move from map to reader

    Once you have identified a document of interest — either by hovering, clicking, or observing its cluster — click it to select it, then click Open in reader in the detail card.

    The app switches to the Close Reading view with the document already loaded. From here, use the Analysis panel’s Document tab to see the narrative arc and sentiment profile, and the Corpus tab to compare it to the rest of your corpus.

    Tip

    To quickly compare two documents that sit close together in the semantic map, open the first in the reader, then return to the Semantic view, select the second, and open it in compare mode from the reader’s toolbar.

Reading the map: worked example

Suppose you load a corpus of British Modernist fiction and poetry. After running the pipeline and enabling clustering at ε = 1.5, you might observe:

  • A tight cluster of Virginia Woolf’s novels, separate from a cluster containing D.H. Lawrence’s novels — their prose rhythms differ significantly even though both write in the same period.
  • T.S. Eliot’s poems as noise points, floating between clusters — poetry embeds differently from prose and rarely joins a prose cluster.
  • Joseph Conrad’s Heart of Darkness sitting closer to Lawrence than to Woolf, which matches critics’ observations about Conrad’s muscular, declarative sentence structure.

None of this is ground truth — the map reflects the model’s learned representation of English prose, not a fixed canonical ranking. Use it as a starting point for further investigation, not as an endpoint.

Troubleshooting

The cloud is flat or looks two-dimensional.
UMAP’s three-dimensional projection can sometimes produce a flat disc when the corpus is very homogeneous (all documents by one author, all from one genre). Increase the corpus size and diversity for better three-dimensional structure.

All points are the same cluster at my ε value.
The epsilon value is too large. Drag the slider left until the cluster count starts to decrease and individual groups become visible.

The auto-rotation makes it hard to inspect points.
Press the pause button in the toolbar to stop rotation. Resume it when you want to see the cloud from a different angle.
